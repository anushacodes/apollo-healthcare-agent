# Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

## Overview
Systemic lupus erythematosus is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune
system produces autoantibodies that attack the body's own tissues, causing
inflammation across multiple organ systems. It disproportionately affects women
of childbearing age and follows a relapsing-remitting course, alternating
between flares and periods of remission.

## Diagnostic Criteria
Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical features and serology rather than
a single test. Commonly used criteria weigh malar rash, discoid rash,
photosensitivity, oral ulcers, non-erosive arthritis, serositis (pleuritis or
pericarditis), renal involvement (persistent proteinuria or cellular casts),
neurologic disorder, hematologic disorder (hemolytic anemia, leukopenia,
lymphopenia, or thrombocytopenia), and positive immunologic markers such as
anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, or antiphospholipid antibodies. A positive ANA is present
in the vast majority of patients but is not specific on its own.

## Common Manifestations
- Constitutional: fatigue, low-grade fever, weight loss.
- Mucocutaneous: malar ("butterfly") rash, discoid lesions, photosensitivity,
  oral or nasal ulcers.
- Musculoskeletal: symmetric, non-erosive arthralgia or arthritis.
- Renal: lupus nephritis is a major driver of long-term morbidity (see
  dedicated document on lupus nephritis management).
- Hematologic: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia.
- Serosal: pleuritic chest pain, pericarditis.
- Neuropsychiatric: ranges from mild cognitive complaints to seizures or
  psychosis in severe cases.

## Monitoring
Patients require regular monitoring of complement levels (C3/C4), anti-dsDNA
titers, complete blood count, renal function, and urinalysis, since these
trend with disease activity and can flag an impending flare before symptoms
become severe.

## General Management Principles
- Hydroxychloroquine is considered baseline background therapy for nearly all
  SLE patients unless contraindicated, and reduces flare frequency and
  long-term organ damage.
- Corticosteroids are used for flare control but chronic high-dose use is
  minimized due to cumulative toxicity (osteoporosis, avascular necrosis,
  metabolic effects).
- Steroid-sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil,
  methotrexate) are added for moderate-to-severe or steroid-dependent disease.
- Biologic therapies (e.g., belimumab, anifrolumab) are options for refractory
  disease not controlled on standard therapy.
- Photoprotection and cardiovascular risk-factor management are important
  adjuncts given the elevated cardiovascular risk in SLE patients independent
  of traditional risk factors.
