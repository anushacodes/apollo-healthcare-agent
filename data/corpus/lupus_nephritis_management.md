# Lupus Nephritis Management

## Overview
Lupus nephritis is renal involvement in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and
is one of the most serious manifestations of the disease, occurring in roughly
a third to half of SLE patients. Untreated or under-treated disease can
progress to end-stage renal disease, making early recognition and biopsy-guided
classification essential.

## Classification
Renal biopsy is the standard for classifying lupus nephritis into one of six
ISN/RPS classes:
- Class I: minimal mesangial disease.
- Class II: mesangial proliferative disease.
- Class III: focal proliferative nephritis (less than 50% of glomeruli
  involved).
- Class IV: diffuse proliferative nephritis (50% or more of glomeruli
  involved) — the most severe and common biopsy finding in patients requiring
  aggressive treatment.
- Class V: membranous nephropathy, often presenting with nephrotic-range
  proteinuria.
- Class VI: advanced sclerosing nephritis with extensive irreversible scarring.

## Clinical Presentation
- Proteinuria, often detected first on routine urinalysis or a spot
  urine protein-to-creatinine ratio.
- Microscopic hematuria and cellular casts on urine microscopy.
- Rising creatinine and declining eGFR in more advanced disease.
- Hypertension frequently accompanies active nephritis.
- Hypocomplementemia (low C3/C4) and rising anti-dsDNA titers often track
  with renal flares.

## Induction Therapy (Class III/IV)
- High-dose corticosteroids (often pulsed IV methylprednisolone followed by
  oral taper) combined with either mycophenolate mofetil or cyclophosphamide
  is standard induction therapy for proliferative disease.
- Belimumab or voclosporin may be added to standard induction regimens in
  patients with an inadequate response, per more recent treatment protocols.

## Maintenance Therapy
- Mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine is continued for maintenance,
  generally for a minimum of several years, with the goal of preventing
  relapse while minimizing cumulative steroid exposure.
- Hydroxychloroquine should be continued throughout, as it reduces the risk of
  renal flares independent of other immunosuppression.

## Monitoring
Repeat urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, creatinine, complement levels, and
anti-dsDNA titers are used to track treatment response, generally at intervals
of every 2-4 weeks during induction and then less frequently once disease is
controlled. A repeat biopsy may be considered if response is inadequate or if
a flare is suspected to have changed histologic class.
