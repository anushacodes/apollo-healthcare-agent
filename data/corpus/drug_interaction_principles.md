# General Principles of Drug-Drug Interaction Assessment

## Overview
Drug-drug interactions occur when one medication alters the effect of
another, either by changing its concentration (pharmacokinetic interaction)
or by amplifying or opposing its physiological effect (pharmacodynamic
interaction). Polypharmacy — common in patients managing multiple chronic
conditions — substantially raises the likelihood of clinically significant
interactions.

## Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- Enzyme inhibition (e.g., a CYP3A4 inhibitor raising levels of a
  co-administered CYP3A4 substrate) can cause toxicity from an otherwise
  standard dose.
- Enzyme induction has the opposite effect, potentially causing
  sub-therapeutic drug levels and treatment failure.
- Interactions affecting absorption, protein binding, or renal clearance can
  also meaningfully change effective drug exposure, particularly for
  narrow-therapeutic-index drugs such as warfarin, digoxin, and certain
  immunosuppressants.

## Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- Additive effects occur when two drugs act on the same physiological
  pathway, such as combining multiple agents that prolong the QT interval, or
  combining anticoagulants with antiplatelet agents to increase bleeding
  risk.
- Opposing effects occur when one drug blunts the intended effect of another,
  such as NSAIDs reducing the antihypertensive effect of ACE inhibitors or
  diuretics.

## High-Risk Combinations in Chronic Disease Management
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs, heparin products) combined with
  antiplatelet agents or NSAIDs substantially increase bleeding risk and
  require careful risk-benefit assessment.
- ACE inhibitors or ARBs combined with potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g.,
  spironolactone) or potassium supplements increase hyperkalemia risk and
  require monitoring of renal function and potassium after initiation or
  dose changes.
- Beta-blockers in patients with reactive airway disease (asthma or COPD)
  require cardioselective agents where possible, with monitoring for
  bronchospasm.
- Metformin held around iodinated contrast administration or during acute
  illness with hemodynamic instability, due to lactic acidosis risk in the
  setting of impaired renal clearance.
- Combining multiple QT-prolonging agents (certain antiarrhythmics,
  antipsychotics, some antibiotics) increases torsades de pointes risk and
  generally warrants ECG monitoring or avoidance where alternatives exist.

## Approach to Interaction Review
A systematic interaction review considers: (1) the mechanism of each
medication, (2) whether the patient's organ function (renal, hepatic) alters
expected drug clearance, (3) whether a narrow-therapeutic-index drug is
involved, and (4) whether the combination is being used deliberately for
synergistic benefit under monitoring (as with some combination antihypertensive
or anticoagulant-antiplatelet regimens in specific cardiovascular
indications) versus being an unintended and avoidable interaction.
