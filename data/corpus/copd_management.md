# COPD Management

## Overview
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive, largely
irreversible airflow limitation caused by chronic inflammation of the airways
and lung parenchyma, most commonly due to cigarette smoking. It encompasses
chronic bronchitis and emphysema phenotypes and is a leading cause of
morbidity worldwide.

## Diagnosis and Staging
Diagnosis requires spirometry demonstrating a post-bronchodilator
FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70 in a patient with compatible symptoms and risk
factor exposure. Severity (GOLD stage) is graded by percent-predicted FEV1:
- GOLD I (mild): FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted.
- GOLD II (moderate): 50-79% predicted.
- GOLD III (severe): 30-49% predicted.
- GOLD IV (very severe): below 30% predicted.

## Symptoms and Exacerbations
Core symptoms are chronic productive cough, exertional dyspnea, and wheeze.
Exacerbations — acute worsening of symptoms beyond normal day-to-day
variation — are a major driver of hospitalization and accelerate disease
progression; frequent exacerbators warrant escalation of maintenance therapy.

## Maintenance Pharmacotherapy
- Long-acting bronchodilators (LAMA and/or LABA) form the backbone of
  maintenance therapy for symptomatic patients above GOLD group A.
- Inhaled corticosteroids are added in patients with frequent exacerbations
  and blood eosinophil counts above threshold, generally as part of a
  triple-therapy inhaler (LAMA/LABA/ICS).
- Roflumilast or chronic macrolide therapy may be considered in select
  patients with chronic bronchitis phenotype and recurrent exacerbations
  despite optimized inhaler therapy.
- Smoking cessation is the single intervention proven to slow disease
  progression and should be addressed at every visit.

## Managing Acute Exacerbations
- Short-acting bronchodilators (SABA +/- SAMA) are first-line for acute
  symptom relief.
- Systemic corticosteroids (typically a short course, 5 days) reduce
  treatment failure and shorten recovery time.
- Antibiotics are indicated when exacerbations feature increased sputum
  purulence combined with increased volume or dyspnea.
- Non-invasive ventilation should be considered early in exacerbations with
  respiratory acidosis to avoid intubation.

## Comorbidity Considerations
COPD frequently coexists with cardiovascular disease, and beta-blockers used
for cardiac indications (e.g., carvedilol in heart failure) should generally
not be withheld solely for COPD, though cardioselective agents are preferred
and patients should be monitored for bronchospasm. Coexisting heart failure
and COPD symptoms overlap substantially (dyspnea, oedema), so objective
testing (spirometry, echocardiography, BNP) is important to disentangle the
relative contribution of each condition to a patient's presentation.
