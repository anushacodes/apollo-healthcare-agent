# Pulmonary Embolism Management

## Overview
Pulmonary embolism (PE) occurs when a thrombus, usually originating from the
deep veins of the legs or pelvis, lodges in the pulmonary arterial
circulation. It ranges in severity from incidental, asymptomatic findings to
massive PE causing hemodynamic collapse and death, so risk stratification is
central to management.

## Clinical Presentation and Pretest Probability
Common symptoms include acute dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, and
occasionally hemoptysis. Clinical prediction rules such as the Wells score for
PE combine risk factors (prior VTE, recent surgery or immobilization, active
malignancy) and exam findings to stratify pretest probability and guide
whether D-dimer testing or direct imaging is appropriate.

## Diagnosis
- D-dimer is a sensitive but non-specific test, most useful for ruling out PE
  in patients with low-to-moderate pretest probability; a negative result in
  this group makes PE unlikely.
- CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the diagnostic standard for confirming
  PE, showing a filling defect in the pulmonary arterial tree.
- Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scanning is an alternative when CTPA is
  contraindicated, such as in significant renal impairment or contrast
  allergy.

## Risk Stratification
Patients with PE are stratified into risk categories based on hemodynamic
status, right ventricular strain on imaging or echocardiography, and cardiac
biomarkers (troponin, BNP):
- High-risk (massive) PE: hemodynamic instability (sustained hypotension) —
  candidates for systemic thrombolysis or catheter-directed therapy.
- Intermediate-risk (submassive) PE: normotensive but with evidence of right
  ventricular strain or elevated biomarkers — managed with anticoagulation
  and close monitoring, with escalation considered if deterioration occurs.
- Low-risk PE: normotensive without RV strain — anticoagulation alone,
  potentially with early outpatient management in appropriate candidates.

## Anticoagulation
Therapeutic anticoagulation is the cornerstone of treatment for the majority
of patients. Options include low molecular weight heparin (e.g., enoxaparin),
unfractionated heparin (preferred in renal impairment or when rapid
reversibility is needed), and direct oral anticoagulants, which are now
frequently used first-line in hemodynamically stable patients without active
cancer or antiphospholipid syndrome. Typical duration is at least three
months, with indefinite anticoagulation considered for unprovoked PE or
persistent risk factors, weighed against ongoing bleeding risk.

## Special Considerations
- Post-operative patients are at elevated VTE risk and often require
  extended pharmacologic prophylaxis in addition to mechanical measures.
- A history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) requires use of a
  non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., fondaparinux, argatroban, or a direct oral
  anticoagulant) rather than heparin or LMWH.
- Thrombophilia workup (including antiphospholipid antibody testing) should
  generally be deferred until the patient is stable and, where possible,
  performed before or well after the acute anticoagulation period, since
  acute thrombosis and anticoagulation itself can transiently alter some
  assay results.
