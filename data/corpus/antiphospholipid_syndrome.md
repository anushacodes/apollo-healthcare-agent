# Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)

## Overview
Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune, hypercoagulable disorder
characterized by recurrent thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity in the
presence of persistently positive antiphospholipid antibodies. It occurs
either as a primary condition or secondary to another autoimmune disease,
most commonly systemic lupus erythematosus.

## Diagnostic Criteria
Diagnosis requires at least one clinical criterion (vascular thrombosis or
defined pregnancy morbidity) plus at least one laboratory criterion, confirmed
on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart:
- Lupus anticoagulant.
- Anticardiolipin antibodies (IgG or IgM) at moderate-to-high titer.
- Anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibodies (IgG or IgM) at moderate-to-high
  titer.

## Clinical Manifestations
- Venous thromboembolism: deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are the
  most common presentations.
- Arterial thrombosis: stroke and transient ischemic attack are the most
  frequent arterial events; myocardial infarction can also occur.
- Obstetric morbidity: recurrent early miscarriage, late fetal death,
  pre-eclampsia, or placental insufficiency leading to preterm delivery.
- Catastrophic APS is a rare but life-threatening variant involving
  widespread microthrombosis and multi-organ failure over a short period.

## Management
- Long-term anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) targeting
  an INR of 2-3 is standard for patients with venous thrombosis; a higher
  target (2.5-3.5) or the addition of aspirin is sometimes used for arterial
  events or recurrent thrombosis on standard-intensity anticoagulation.
- Direct oral anticoagulants are generally avoided in patients with
  triple-positive antibody profiles or prior arterial events, as trial data
  showed higher recurrence rates compared with warfarin in this population.
- Low-dose aspirin is used for primary thromboprophylaxis in asymptomatic
  antibody-positive individuals with a secondary risk factor, and is
  frequently combined with prophylactic heparin during pregnancy.
- Obstetric APS during pregnancy is typically managed with low-dose aspirin
  plus prophylactic-dose heparin to reduce miscarriage risk.

## Considerations in Patients with Overlapping SLE
Patients with SLE and secondary APS require coordinated management: SLE
disease-activity control (hydroxychloroquine, immunosuppression as needed)
alongside APS-directed anticoagulation. Hydroxychloroquine itself has
antithrombotic properties and is recommended in this overlap population
regardless of lupus activity.
